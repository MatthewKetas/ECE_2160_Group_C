Evaluating Power Reduction for the Critter Embedded System
Matthew KetasDept. of Electrical and Computer EngineeringSwanson School of Engineering, University of PittsburghPittsburgh, PA, USAmtk67@pitt.edu
Abstract—This case study evaluates a five-step approach to reducing power consumption in the Critter, a Raspberry Pi 5 prototype for unattended machine-room temperature monitoring. The approach examines CPU core availability, frequency governor, maximum clock frequency, assembly optimization, and unused peripherals while retaining the three-unit I/O, memory, and prediction structure. Eighty-four runs across sixteen conditions were measured through the power management integrated circuit over consistent 60-second windows. The combined configuration reduced mean measured rail power from 1.882696 W to 1.614476 W, a 14.25% reduction. The complete assembly variant produced a 10.22% reduction, the largest observed reduction among separately evaluated full-program treatments. Memory showed a 19.03% reduction relative to its own baseline, whereas I/O evidence was inconclusive and the single-call computation experiment could not resolve prediction energy. The results support further validation of a low-power Critter configuration, but do not establish total device energy, timing compliance, or prediction accuracy. Deployment decisions must preserve fresh temperature measurements, useful history, and timely reporting of HVAC behavior.
Keywords—embedded systems, Critter, Raspberry Pi 5, PMIC, power measurement, assembly optimization
I. Introduction
With all power saving techniques applied, the combined Critter configuration used 0.268220 W less mean PMIC rail power than the original full-program configuration over a 60-second measurement window. This represents a 14.25% reduction, with estimated window energy decreasing from 112.961768 J to 96.868548 J [4]. Reducing unnecessary software operations and matching the platform configuration to the workload produced a measurable benefit. The next question is whether that benefit can be retained while the deployed product continues to monitor a room reliably.
This work was completed for ECE 2160, Embedded Computer System Design, at the University of Pittsburgh. The course connects embedded implementation with C, assembly, data structures, I/O, and real-time behavior [1]. The power assignment requires evaluating device configuration and edited assembly using Critter’s three prototype units [2]. Each unit of the Critter exists as a pipeline within the monitoring product: I/O supplies fresh data, memory retains useful evidence, and computation produces meaningful HVAC estimates. A configuration that saves power by losing measurements would not satisfy that purpose.
Section II establishes the product and measurement context. Section III describes the five-step approach and the trials. Section IV presents the workbook data and charts. Section V explains the configuration results, all nine assembly changes, and the implications for Critter and embedded systems as a whole. Section VI concludes with further study required.
II. Background
A.  The Critter and the evaluation objective
The Critter supports temperature monitoring across distributed machine rooms and preliminary identification of heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning (HVAC) issues. The implementation evaluated here represents one independently operating device in one room. I/O acquires and saves measurements; memory validates, filters, and summarizes recent samples; computation estimates temperature trends and extrapolates a prediction 30 seconds ahead. The Pilot reports information and does not control HVAC equipment [2]–[4].
The original program is single-threaded. During approximately one minute of acquisition, it reads a sample, saves it, passes it to the memory unit, and requests a 100 ms sleep. Processing time is added to that delay, so 10 Hz is a nominal target rather than a guaranteed sampling rate. Memory retains up to 100 accepted samples and uses median absolute deviation to detect outliers. Computation consumes a summary after acquisition. Although the assignment distinguishes I/O, memory, and computational behavior, this implementation’s prediction function is brief and its memory working set is small [3], [4]. Those properties limit what the prototype can tell us about a future intensive forecasting workload.
My previous safety paper identified 49 confirmed finding records involving data persistence, source identity, buffer state, numeric behavior, and other concerns [3]. That history matters here because power optimization must preserve the behavior we intend to keep without treating existing defects as acceptable requirements. Matching the original assembly output establishes regression evidence for tested inputs; it does not establish correct HVAC predictions or resolve every safety finding.
B.  Power measurement and reduction tactics
The Raspberry Pi 5 PMIC exposes paired voltage and current readings for twelve monitored rails. Summing their voltage and current products gives a useful rail-power estimate, but it excludes loads outside those rails and is not a measurement of total USB-C input power. The assignment’s forum discussion and RPi5-power example motivated this measurement method [5], [6]. 
The evaluated tactics change how much processing capacity remains available, the requested CPU operating frequency, and the instructions used for a fixed operation. Linux’s generic performance governor requests the highest frequency permitted by the policy, while powersave requests the lowest permitted frequency [7]. A 1500 MHz maximum therefore does not mean that powersave runs continuously at 1500 MHz. Unused radios can also be disabled in this standalone configuration, but a distributed deployment may need a communication path for data retrieval and fault reporting.
Power and energy answer different design questions. Mean power describes the rate of energy use during monitoring. Energy per completed task also depends on execution time. A slower configuration can lower watts while taking longer to finish the same work. This experiment estimates energy for equal monitoring windows, so its power and energy rankings coincide. A separate completed-work experiment is needed before concluding that every reduction in window power saves energy per prediction or per processed dataset.
III. Approach
A.  Measurement procedure and evidence
I used an AI-assisted Python logger to call vcgencmd pmic_​read_​adc once per second for sixty readings. The logger pairs each current with its corresponding voltage, rejects incomplete rail sets, and records elapsed time, command duration, summed rail power, and the underlying rail readings. It schedules reads against a monotonic clock and buffers the CSV output until the measurement ends [4]. This reduces repeated disk output during measurement, but Python and the monitoring command still consume power.
For each run, mean power is the arithmetic mean of its sixty recorded power values. The workbook estimates energy by multiplying that mean by the nominal 60-second duration. The last sample is near 59 seconds because the logger waits after the final reading; that timestamp is not the full window duration. The CSV files omit the final elapsed duration and program start/finish markers. I therefore report PMIC measurement-window energy without subtracting idle power or claiming isolated application energy.
The experimental protocol called for leaving the desktop, display, peripherals, network, and cooling arrangement unchanged except for the intended treatment, allowing the Pi to settle, and avoiding interaction during collection. Ten idle runs and ten full-program baseline runs were collected. Most other conditions have five runs; the retained records contain six two-core runs and six optimized I/O runs, while computation has one run per version. All 84 runs and 5,040 samples were retained. The trials were collected sequentially rather than in randomized blocks [4].
B.  The five-step evaluation process
I organized the work into five checks: reduce available cores while preserving required behavior; select a suitable frequency governor; reduce the maximum frequency; optimize and validate assembly; and disable hardware unnecessary to the intended deployment. These checks are a suggested planning framework, and different embedded systems may also have differing optimal orders. Power-saving treatments were compared against a baseline during experimentation rather than applied as a fully documented cumulative sequence.
For this experiment, each change had a recorded configuration, an expected mechanism/reduction, and a repeatable workload. A promising configuration was then combined with other accepted changes and measured again because individual savings were not always assumed to add. Table I includes every recorded condition. Figures 1–3 retain the eight comparisons from the analysis workbook [4].
C.  Baseline and device configuration trials
The idle condition ran the logger with Critter stopped. The full-program baseline ran the original Pilot alongside the same logger and is labeled as four cores in the workbook. During these runs the Pi was configured with the Ondemand speed governor and a maximum CPU frequency of 2400 MHz. As the Ondemand speed governor was used, the baseline is therefore an observed configuration, not evidence that the Pi remained at maximum frequency throughout every run.
The core-count trials ran the complete program with two cores and then one core available. Linux CPU hotplug provides a reproducible implementation: keep CPU0 online, write 0 to the cpu2/online and cpu3/online controls for two cores, and additionally write 0 to cpu1/online for one core under /sys/​devices/system/​cpu/. Writing 1 restores those cores; the online mask verifies the result [8]. These commands describe how to reproduce the intended settings. The measurement files do not retain the original shell commands or verify physical power gating. The comparison is about available CPU cores, not a claim that every offlined core consumes zero power.
For the frequency trials, the recorded protocol sets scaling_​max_​freq under /sys/​devices/system/​cpu/cpufreq/policy0 to 2100, 1800, and 1500 MHz. It selects performance through scaling_​governor and calls vcgencmd measure_​clock arm, measure_​temp, and get_​throttled for inspection. Five full-program runs were recorded at each cap. A further five runs used powersave with the 1500 MHz maximum. The files preserve the intended condition labels but do not contain continuous clock, temperature, governor, or throttling telemetry [4].
The final five runs combined one available CPU core, a 1500 MHz maximum, powersave, all nine assembly edits, and disabled Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. 
D.  Trials for the three prototype units
The standalone I/O executable reused the Pilot’s acquisition and saving functions, 60-second loop, progress output, and 100 ms delay. Memory processing and prediction were excluded. The comparison contains five baseline and six assembly-optimized runs. Source priority remained Sense HAT, saved data, then CPU-temperature fallback. In this particular case, the CPU temperature was measured as I did not have access to the Sense HAT [4].
The Memory executable replayed the same 600 compiled-in synthetic temperatures, spaced at 0.1-second timestamps, with six injected outliers. It initialized the same 100-sample buffer, processed each input once with a 100 ms pause, and generated a final summary. Expected outcomes were 600 valid inputs, zero invalid inputs, six outliers, and 100 retained samples. The original API counts outliers as valid inputs even though it excludes them from retention. Five runs were collected for each version. This fixed-work replay takes slightly more than a minute, so the 60-second logger may miss its final processing and summary. It measures window power rather than total energy for all 600 inputs.
The computation executable called the actual prediction function once using the fixed Memory summary and a 30-second horizon. The logger started first, followed by a two-second delay and the computation process. The expected output was 25.732950 °C. One baseline and one optimized run were retained. A one-second sampler cannot reliably resolve such a short function call, and most of this measurement window contains background activity rather than prediction work. This trial documents the limitation of the chosen instrumentation for the computational unit.
E.  Assembly preparation and validation
The archived assembly identifies GCC 14.2.0 targeting 64-bit ARM Linux, using C11 and -O0. The units were translated with gcc -std=c11 -O0 -Wall -Wextra -S -fverbose-asm, with the Pilot include directory supplied, and the original output was retained before editing. GCC’s -S option stops before assembly, producing editable assembly source [9]. With the help of AI assistance, nine individual optimizations were found in the assembly that were compatible with the Pilot implementation. The edited files were then assembled and linked with the math library into standalone I/O, Memory, and Computation executables.
The complete optimized Pilot linked the edited unit assembly. Its build script carried the standalone I/O delay change into a generated copy of Pilot’s main function by replacing the runtime delay conversion with the fixed 100 ms timespec before emitting assembly. Thus the integrated trial represents the nine changes, although this main-function change was reproduced through generated C rather than by linking the standalone I/O entry point [4].
The repository’s equivalence test compares original and edited ARM routines, including ring state, summaries, sorting, standard deviation, prediction boundaries, invalid inputs, allocation failures, sensor-register read failures, pressure conversion, and the sleep interval. The harness assembles the Linux files and adapts object-format details for native testing, with sensor reads mocked. This verifies the tested instruction behavior, not Raspberry Pi timing, physical I/O, or product-level correctness.
F.  Statistical comparison
Each CSV is one experimental repetition. The sixty samples within a CSV are used to estimate its mean rather than being counted as sixty independent trials. I report the mean and sample standard deviation across runs. The workbook uses two-sided Welch tests, which allow unequal sample sizes and variances, with a 0.05 threshold [10]. Bonferroni adjustment is applied within five exploratory comparison families: cores (3 tests), clock settings (6), governor (1), estimable assembly comparisons (3), and combined configuration (9). There is no correction across all five families.
The computation comparison is descriptive because one observation per version cannot estimate between-run variance. Power and energy tests are mathematically redundant because each energy value is exactly sixty times the corresponding power. Small p-values describe separation in the recorded runs; they do not eliminate differences in temperature, background activity, input source, or other unrecorded conditions across collection dates.

These comparisons support investigating repeated memory processing as an optimization target, but the standalone unit values cannot be added to reconstruct the whole-program value because they contain shared platform power and different workload schedules.
The computation panel’s apparent 20.63% decrease describes two entire monitoring windows. It is not a measurement of a 20.63% reduction in prediction energy. Its unusually high baseline, the short single call, and the lack of repeated runs make it unsuitable for ranking computation against I/O or Memory. The numerical values are included for completeness without presenting them as an established optimization result.
V. Results
A.  Effects of cores and frequency policy
Reducing available cores from the labeled four-core baseline to one reduced mean power by 6.86%, or 0.129078 W; two cores reduced it by 6.77%, or 0.127476 W. The adjusted p-values were 0.0000149 and 0.00000288, respectively. The one-versus-two-core difference was only 0.001602 W and was not statistically resolved (adjusted p = 1.000). These data support testing fewer cores, but do not establish one core as superior to two.
For Critter, this is plausible because the prototype’s three functional units execute sequentially rather than on three dedicated threads. However, the operating system, logger, file writes, and future communication work still need CPU time. I would retain one core as a candidate for the current standalone workload and keep two cores available as an alternative if timing tests show contention. Adding parallel prediction or network services would require measuring the revised workload again.
At 1500 MHz, mean power was 5.71% below baseline (adjusted p = 0.0000433). The 1800 MHz condition was only 0.86% below baseline and inconclusive (adjusted p = 1.000). The 2100 MHz condition was 4.89% above baseline (adjusted p = 0.000270). Thus, the data do not show that every clock change reduced power. Among the explicitly tested caps, 1500 MHz was lower than both 1800 and 2100 MHz after adjustment. The baseline’s unknown frequency policy prevents interpreting the 2100 MHz result as an anomaly in a controlled frequency sweep.
Selecting powersave at the 1500 MHz maximum reduced the mean from 1.775101 to 1.712785 W, an additional 3.51% relative to the performance-protocol condition (p = 0.000171). Relative to the original full-program baseline, this configuration was 9.02% lower. Only these two governor conditions were measured. The result identifies a promising policy for this paced workload, not the best governor for every Critter workload or a guarantee that an intensive future prediction will finish on time. This result was also particularly surprising as setting the 1500 MHz cap should have eliminated the purpose of a governor as that is also the lowest possible clock speed on the Pi. However, even with the 1500 MHz ceiling applied, changing the governor to powersave reduced power significantly. 
B.  The nine assembly optimization cases
The following cases describe the actual differences between the archived original and edited assembly [4]. Their mechanisms explain why the changes were worth testing. Individual instruction sequences were not measured separately, so no case is assigned its own power-saving percentage.
1) Fixed I/O delay construction. IO_​main.s originally loaded the constant 0.1-second interval and repeatedly converted it between floating-point and integer representations to construct a timespec. The edited sequence loads 100000000 and uses STP to store zero seconds and 100000000 nanoseconds together. This removes conversion and arithmetic work on each acquisition iteration while preserving the requested sleep. It is valid for the fixed 100 ms interval; changing the configured interval requires updating the sequence. It does not change sensor sampling requirements or remove the delay.
2) Pressure conversion. In sense_​hat_​environment_​read_​pressure, the original code assembled a 24-bit reading, branched for sign extension, converted it to double, and divided by 4096. The edited code uses SBFX to sign-extend the 24 bits and SCVTF with twelve fractional bits to perform the scaled conversion. This preserves the baseline’s raw-value interpretation and error returns while removing an explicit floating-point division and sign-handling branch. It benefits the I/O path only when pressure acquisition executes; a temperature-only fallback would not exercise it.
3) Median calculation. critter_​median retains the original exchange-sort comparison and swap order but keeps pointers, loop state, and values in registers instead of repeatedly spilling them to the stack. Pointer increments and shifted addressing locate doubles; a right shift finds the middle index. For an even count, multiplication by 0.5 replaces division by 2. Empty-input behavior and the two-middle-value averaging rule remain. This improves a routine used repeatedly by outlier detection without changing the sorting algorithm or claiming better asymptotic complexity.
4) Standard deviation. critter_​stddev uses a register-held accumulator and a post-incrementing load through the input array. It retains the original scalar subtraction, squaring, and addition order, then divides by the sample count. A tail branch to the library sqrt routine avoids a separate return frame while retaining the library operation. Preserving accumulation order matters because changed rounding could alter a later HVAC threshold. This case reduces loop and stack traffic rather than replacing the statistical definition.
5) Ring insertion and copying. critter_​add_​in_​ring replaces division-based remainder for advancing the head with increment, comparison, and conditional selection of zero at the capacity boundary. It conditionally increments the count until full. Shifted addition forms the 40-byte sample address, and paired loads and stores transfer the sample’s first 32 bytes before the remaining eight bytes. The optimization depends on an initialized ring with head below capacity and count no greater than capacity. It preserves normal buffer behavior but is not new protection against corrupted ring metadata.
6) Outlier-buffer traversal. critter_​detect_​outlier computes the oldest sample index once from head and count, conditionally adding capacity when needed. It then walks the ring by incrementing and wrapping, rather than recomputing a division-based remainder for every temperature copied to the temporary array. The chronological selection of samples remains the same. Because filtering occurs repeatedly during acquisition, removing repeated indexing work can affect more of the monitoring window than optimizing a prediction called only once.
7) Paired absolute deviations. The same outlier routine uses NEON vector subtraction and absolute value to calculate two double-precision deviations from the median per iteration. A scalar tail handles an odd number of samples. Each deviation is independent, so the edit does not reorder a floating-point sum. The subsequent median calculation and the original outlier threshold of three times median absolute deviation plus 0.5 °C remain. This reduces loop work while preserving which inputs the baseline retains for the tested datasets.
8) Summary construction. critter_​memory_​build_​summary keeps the running sum, minimum, and maximum in registers, advances through the ring without repeated division, and resolves the latest sample once for both its timestamp and temperature. Conditional floating-point selections replace branches for extrema, while paired operations move adjacent fields. The original sum order, source-state transitions, allocation behavior, and calls to median and standard deviation are preserved. For Critter, consistent summary fields are essential because every subsequent trend estimate depends on them. Preserving source transitions also means that this optimization does not itself repair the earlier source-identity findings.
9) Prediction and HVAC flags. critter_​compute_​analysis becomes a leaf routine that holds input pointers and intermediate values in caller-saved registers instead of an 80-byte stack frame. It reuses the computed slope and absolute slope and uses conditional comparisons and selections for threshold flags and the elapsed-time fallback. Separate multiply and add operations preserve the baseline rounding behavior rather than introducing fused arithmetic. Tested null-input, empty-summary, and nonfinite-horizon behavior is retained. The result is fewer loads, stores, and branches, but the current power experiment cannot resolve this one call’s energy or establish the model’s forecasting accuracy.
C.  Measured assembly effects and their limits
Memory’s mean fell by 19.03% relative to its own baseline, with adjusted p = 3.03 × 10⁻⁹. I/O’s mean fell by 3.69%, but the adjusted p-value was 0.209, so the retained runs do not establish an I/O reduction at the selected threshold. Computation has no estimable p-value. The complete Pilot fell by 10.22%, with adjusted p = 6.83 × 10⁻⁸. Assembly optimization was therefore the largest observed reduction among the separately evaluated full-program treatments, while the combined treatment was lower still.
The Memory result is consistent with repeated sorting, copying, and outlier processing making redundant instructions worth removing. It does not identify the fraction saved by NEON, ring indexing, or median alone. Nor does it show that the 100-sample workload saturates memory bandwidth. The complete assembly experiment compares edited code against -O0 output, where GCC leaves most optimizations disabled [9]. A practical next comparison is validated -O2 or -Os C against the hand edits. The current data cannot establish that maintaining custom assembly provides a benefit over normal compiler optimization.
D.  Combined configuration and Critter deployment
The combined configuration reached 1.614476 W and 96.868548 J per nominal window, 14.25% below the original baseline (adjusted p = 4.66 × 10⁻⁸). It was lower than every measured full-program alternative within the workbook’s combined-comparison family. It also used 0.075853 W less than the assembly-only configuration, a 4.49% reduction relative to that configuration (adjusted p = 0.000272). The extra reduction includes the combined effect of configuration and radios; it cannot be attributed to a single remaining step. Adding the percentages from separate trials would count overlapping effects and use inconsistent denominators.
For an unattended Critter installation, the practical value is reduced energy use during monitoring, provided the same useful measurements and reports are delivered. As an illustration only, sustaining the observed 0.268220 W difference continuously for one year would save about 2.35 kWh in the measured rails per device, or about 235 kWh across 100 devices. This extrapolation assumes unchanged operating conditions and duty cycle. It is not an observed annual saving, a wall-power estimate, or a battery-life prediction.
Disabling Wi-Fi and Bluetooth is compatible with this local, independently operating prototype if data can be retrieved through an acceptable alternative. For a distributed installation that relies on wireless telemetry, permanently disabling its only link would prevent remote monitoring. Future experiments would instead need to evaluate scheduled transfers, local buffering during outages, and the delay allowed before an HVAC concern reaches an operator. The required sensor interface, persistent storage, and communication path must remain available whenever the product needs them.
E.  Acceptance criteria and further experiments
The absence of HVAC actuation does not make timing irrelevant. A slower acquisition loop provides fewer observations per minute, and a 100-sample buffer then spans a different length of room history. If a hot excursion is missed or a changing temperature is repeatedly rejected as an outlier, lower power would come at the expense of the product’s purpose. The current power logs do not contain the per-configuration sample counts, gaps, dropped samples, reporting latency, or prediction errors needed to certify that requirements were met.
Before selecting the final configuration, “high rate” and “timely prediction” should be quantified with  real limits for accepted samples per second, maximum acquisition gap, age of the newest summary data, and delay before a report is available. Replaying known temperature ramps and HVAC cycling traces should check retained samples, source labels, summaries, and predictions. Missing sensors, failed writes, and loss of the communication path should be included because they are directly connected to the prior safety findings [3]. Those are proposed acceptance checks, not outcomes demonstrated by this dataset.
The next measurement campaign should randomize or alternate baseline and candidate runs while saving the online-core mask, actual frequency, governor limits, input source, temperatures, throttling state, and program markers. Each configuration needs its own idle reference if incremental workload power is studied. An external input-power meter can test total device savings and quantify monitoring overhead. Memory energy should cover all 600 inputs and the final summary. Computation should be repeated in a controlled batch long enough to measure, with outputs checked and loop overhead accounted for, then assessed in its expected deployment duty cycle.
Finally, a radio-only trial would isolate the peripheral contribution, and a larger memory history or a more intensive predictor would test whether the chosen configuration scales to the course product’s intended behavior. The five-step process remains useful because it makes these decisions explicit. Its outcome is a configuration supported by workload and requirement evidence, rather than a universal rule to minimize cores and frequency on every embedded system.
VI. Conclusion
The Critter trials demonstrate a 14.25% reduction in mean measured PMIC rail power for a combined configuration using one core, a 1500 MHz maximum, powersave, nine assembly edits, and disabled Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. Assembly optimization alone produced a 10.22% full-program reduction, the largest observed reduction among the separately evaluated full-program treatments. The strong memory result helps identify repeated data processing as a useful target in this prototype; the I/O and computation results require more evidence.
For Critter, the appropriate next step is to validate the combined configuration against acquisition continuity, retained history, prediction behavior, and the deployment’s reporting needs. The measurements support that candidate without proving timing compliance, accurate forecasts, or total input-energy savings. The five-step approach can guide future embedded systems work, but each step must remain conditional on the product’s requirements. An optimization is valuable only when the unattended system continues to deliver the temperature evidence needed to identify HVAC problems.

Source Outlines
Course context [1], [2]. The public Pitt catalog establishes the course title and its embedded implementation topics. The supplied assignment establishes the three Critter units and required power-reduction options. 
Prior safety paper [3]. The paper follows Introduction, Background, Approach, Data, Results, Conclusion, References, and Acknowledgements. Its product description, nominal acquisition rate, buffer structure, and findings about source identity and prediction behavior inform this study. The present paper uses that work to define what optimization must preserve, not as evidence that the power variants are safe or accurate.
Experimental evidence [4]. The workbook provides run counts, power and window-energy summaries, variability, Welch tests, and comparison families. The source code and assembly establish what each unit and edit actually does. These materials support the reported results but lack continuous configuration telemetry and per-run functional performance records.
Measurement background [5], [6]. The forum is retained as an assignment-provided pointer. The RPi5-power author’s README documents the twelve-rail summation and its limits relative to input power. Its calibration constants are specific to its reported setup and are not imported into this study.
Implementation and statistics [7]–[10]. Linux documentation supports the governor and core-control descriptions. GCC documentation supports assembly generation and the unoptimized baseline distinction. NIST supports the unequal-variance two-sample comparison. These sources explain mechanisms and methods; the measured percentages come from the Critter records.
